## Why It Is Included

This page is included as a vehicle-example companion to the Dieselgate controversy, not as a review of any specific Volkswagen, Audi, or Porsche model. The historically relevant subject is the interaction between diesel emissions hardware, engine control software, laboratory certification, real-world operation, and regulatory findings.

EPA materials describe Volkswagen diesel vehicles that used software behavior regulators identified as defeat devices. EPA settlement material documents the civil-remedy context, while DOJ material covers the later criminal sentencing. ICCT-supported real-world testing helped bring attention to the gap between laboratory certification expectations and on-road emissions behavior. This page keeps those source roles separate: independent testing raised technical questions, EPA made regulatory findings, and DOJ handled criminal enforcement.

## Technologies Illustrated

Affected Volkswagen TDI vehicles are useful examples for studying diesel combustion, NOx control, aftertreatment, ECU calibration, onboard diagnostics, and regulatory definitions of defeat devices.

The technical chain matters because diesel emissions control is not one part:

- **Combustion behavior:** Diesel engines often operate lean, with excess oxygen. This can help efficiency but complicates NOx treatment compared with stoichiometric gasoline engines.
- **Injection and air handling:** Turbocharging, exhaust-gas recirculation, and injection timing affect torque, soot, combustion temperature, and NOx formation.
- **Aftertreatment:** Diesel oxidation catalysts, particulate filters, selective catalytic reduction, lean NOx traps, sensors, and reductant dosing can all be part of the system depending on engine family and market.
- **ECU calibration:** Software decides how aggressively emissions controls operate under different conditions.
- **Certification boundary:** If a vehicle behaves differently because it recognizes a test cycle, the laboratory result no longer represents the system's ordinary operating behavior.

This is why the example belongs in the vehicle section. The controversy was not merely about an abstract regulation. It was about production vehicles whose hardware and software together determined real emissions behavior.

## Historical and Regulatory Context

Passenger diesel vehicles in the 2000s and early 2010s were often discussed as efficient alternatives to gasoline vehicles in some markets. The underlying engineering tradeoff was difficult: lower fuel consumption, customer drivability, aftertreatment durability, urea consumption, cost, and NOx control could pull calibration decisions in different directions.

Those tradeoffs do not justify illegal defeat devices. They do explain why modern diesel systems became heavily dependent on software, sensors, and test design. The vehicle example therefore connects old mechanical diesel history to the software-defined vehicle era. A calibration map can become a regulated behavior, not merely an internal engineering setting.

EPA settlement material describes remedies that included buybacks, modifications where approved, and mitigation obligations. DOJ sentencing material describes criminal consequences for the company. Those outcomes should be attributed precisely; this page should not turn regulatory findings into unsupported claims about every diesel vehicle or every manufacturer.

## Neutral Reading

The page deliberately avoids treating TDI vehicles as product recommendations, brand condemnations, or diesel technology rankings. Volkswagen TDI vehicles are included because they illustrate how certification, software behavior, emissions hardware, and public trust became inseparable. The neutral lesson is that a vehicle's environmental claim depends on in-use behavior, not only on the presence of aftertreatment hardware or a laboratory compliance result.

This example also should not imply that all diesel technology is equivalent to Dieselgate. Modern diesel systems still serve roles in passenger, commercial, and heavy-duty applications. The case instead shows that when software controls regulated physical systems, evidence must cover both the hardware and the conditions under which the software operates.
