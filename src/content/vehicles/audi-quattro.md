## Why Included

The Audi Quattro is included because it made permanent all-wheel drive visible in a performance road-car and rally context. Audi's company-history material identifies the Quattro's March 1980 Geneva debut and describes its permanent all-wheel drive with manually lockable differentials. Audi's quattro history also emphasizes a high-speed, lightweight four-wheel-drive system offered in 1980.

The page is not here to rank rally cars. It is here to explain how a drivetrain layout can change traction, packaging, transmission design, weight distribution, and public expectations of all-weather performance.

## Technologies Illustrated

The Quattro illustrates permanent all-wheel drive in a car whose engine and transmission package also had to serve a turbocharged road vehicle. Torque needed to pass through front and rear drive paths, with differential strategy determining how speed differences and traction limits were handled.

Audi's quattro history notes the later move from manually lockable differentials to a Torsen center differential in 1986. That transition is useful because it shows all-wheel drive as an evolving control and mechanical-distribution problem, not a fixed label.

## Historical Role

The Quattro's historical role sits between motorsport and production engineering. Rally competition made the traction benefits highly visible, while the road car showed that permanent all-wheel drive could be packaged for public use in a relatively compact coupe.

A neutral interpretation should keep the claim bounded. The Quattro did not invent four-wheel drive, and it did not make all later AWD systems identical. It is important because it helped move high-speed, full-time all-wheel-drive thinking into road-car discussions and into Audi's long-running brand technology strategy.
