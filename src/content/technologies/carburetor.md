## Definition

A carburetor meters fuel into the intake air stream using pressure differences, jets, and mechanical linkages. It predates electronic fuel injection and was central to gasoline vehicles for much of the twentieth century.

In a basic carbureted gasoline engine, incoming air passes through a narrowed throat that lowers pressure and draws fuel from calibrated passages. The goal is to deliver a combustible air-fuel mixture before it enters the cylinders. The exact mixture depends on airflow, throttle opening, fuel level, temperature, altitude, and mechanical adjustment.

## Why It Emerged

Early spark-ignition engines needed a practical way to vaporize and meter liquid fuel. Carburetors provided a mechanical solution that could be manufactured, adjusted, and repaired with the tools of the period.

The carburetor made sense in an era before inexpensive electronic sensors and microprocessor control. It could combine fuel metering, throttle response, choke operation, idle control, and enrichment for acceleration or load using mechanical passages and linkages. Vehicles such as the Ford Model T help show why this mattered: maintainability and repeatability were central to early mass motorization.

## Components and Behavior

A carburetor can include a float bowl, jets, venturi, throttle plate, choke, idle circuit, accelerator pump, and enrichment circuits. These parts do not measure combustion directly. They infer fuel demand mechanically from airflow, pressure, and driver input.

That inference can work well enough for many operating conditions, but it is not precise in the same way as closed-loop electronic fuel injection. Cold starts may require a richer mixture. High altitude changes air density. Sudden throttle movement may require temporary enrichment. Wear, deposits, and adjustment can all affect mixture.

Different circuits cover different parts of the driving envelope. An idle circuit feeds fuel when airflow through the main venturi is too low for stable metering. A choke enriches the mixture during cold starting because fuel vaporization is poor. An accelerator pump can add fuel during a quick throttle opening before airflow and pressure signals settle. These features made carburetors adaptable, but they also show the limitation: the device responds through calibrated passages and springs rather than direct measurement of exhaust oxygen, catalyst state, or cylinder-by-cylinder combustion.

## Why It Declined

Carburetors were limited in precision across changing temperature, altitude, engine load, and emissions requirements. Electronic fuel injection and oxygen-sensor feedback made fuel control more adaptable.

The decline of carburetors should not be framed as a simple victory of "better" over "worse." Carburetors matched the manufacturing, repair, and regulation context of their era. They became less suitable as emissions limits tightened, catalytic converters needed precise mixture control, and electronic control units could use sensor feedback to adjust fuel delivery more accurately.

## Neutral Reading

This page treats carburetors as historical control devices, not as nostalgic icons or obsolete failures. They solved real problems for early and mid-century vehicles. Their limitations became more consequential when the vehicle system changed around them: stricter emissions rules, higher drivability expectations, more diagnostics, and tighter integration with aftertreatment.
