## Definition

A torque converter is a hydrodynamic coupling between an engine and a transmission. It lets the engine continue running while the vehicle is stopped, provides smooth launch from rest, and can multiply torque under some speed differences between the pump and turbine.

It is most closely associated with conventional automatic transmissions, though similar launch functions can appear in other transmission families.

## How It Works

The engine drives the converter pump, which throws transmission fluid outward. Fluid strikes the turbine connected to the transmission input shaft, causing it to rotate. Between them sits a stator that redirects returning fluid. At launch, the stator can improve torque transfer by changing the fluid path; as pump and turbine speeds converge, the converter behaves more like a fluid coupling.

Because fluid coupling involves slip, it can waste energy as heat. Many converters include a lockup clutch. Once launch smoothness is no longer needed, lockup can mechanically connect the engine and transmission input to reduce slip.

## Main Components

- **Pump or impeller:** driven by the engine.
- **Turbine:** connected to the transmission input.
- **Stator and one-way clutch:** redirect returning fluid during torque multiplication.
- **Lockup clutch:** creates a mechanical connection under selected conditions.
- **Converter housing and fluid:** contain the hydrodynamic circuit and transfer energy.
- **Control valves and software:** manage apply pressure, slip target, and lockup timing.

## Why It Emerged

A combustion engine cannot produce useful wheel torque at zero engine speed and generally cannot remain running if rigidly coupled to a stationary drivetrain. The torque converter solved that launch problem without a driver-operated clutch. It also filtered torsional vibration and made early automatic transmissions practical for everyday road use.

Later, lockup control became important because a continuously slipping converter consumes energy. Electronic controls allowed partial and full lockup strategies to be coordinated with engine load, shift scheduling, and vibration management.

## Tradeoffs and Constraints

Slip helps smooth launch and absorb vibration, but slip also produces heat. Lockup reduces loss but can transmit vibration if applied too early or too firmly. The calibration must manage comfort, durability, thermal limits, fuel consumption, and driveline response.

Torque multiplication is also conditional. A converter does not multiply torque equally at all speeds. Its behavior depends on pump-turbine speed difference, stator action, fluid design, and lockup state.

## Common Misconceptions

A torque converter is not the same thing as an automatic transmission. It is one major coupling device within many automatic layouts. The gear ratios, shift elements, hydraulic controls, and electronic scheduling are separate parts of the complete transmission.
