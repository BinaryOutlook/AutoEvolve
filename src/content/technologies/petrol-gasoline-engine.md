## Definition

A petrol or gasoline engine is an internal-combustion engine that usually uses spark ignition. Air and vaporized fuel are mixed before or during intake, compressed by the piston, and ignited by a spark plug at a controlled crank angle. The expanding gases push the piston and turn the crankshaft.

The broad four-stroke sequence is intake, compression, power, and exhaust. In mathematical shorthand, engine output power still follows the rotational relationship:

$$
P = T\omega
$$

where \(P\) is power, \(T\) is torque, and \(\omega\) is crankshaft angular speed.

## How It Works

In a port-injected gasoline engine, fuel is sprayed into the intake port before entering the cylinder. In a direct-injected engine, fuel is sprayed into the cylinder itself. The throttle and valve events control incoming air, while the engine control unit calculates fuel quantity and ignition timing from sensor inputs.

Because a spark initiates combustion, the air-fuel mixture must be controlled to avoid misfire, knock, and excessive emissions. Three-way catalytic converters also work most effectively when the mixture is managed close to stoichiometric conditions during many operating modes.

## Main Components

- **Cylinder block, pistons, connecting rods, and crankshaft:** convert pressure into rotation.
- **Cylinder head and valves:** control air, fuel mixture, and exhaust flow.
- **Spark plugs and ignition coils:** initiate combustion.
- **Fuel metering system:** carburetor, port injection, or direct injection depending on era and design.
- **Engine control unit:** coordinates fuel, spark, throttle, variable valve timing, and diagnostics.
- **Exhaust aftertreatment:** reduces regulated pollutants after combustion.

## Why It Emerged

Gasoline engines became central to early automobiles because liquid fuel stored substantial energy in a portable form, and spark-ignition engines could be packaged into road vehicles with tanks, carburetion, cooling, lubrication, and mechanical drivetrains. Early gasoline vehicles competed with steam and battery-electric designs before the gasoline supply chain, electric starting, mass production, and road networks reinforced their role.

## Tradeoffs and Constraints

Gasoline evaporates readily enough to support mixture formation, but that volatility also requires vapor control and careful safety design. Spark-ignition engines can respond quickly to throttle changes, yet they are constrained by knock, pumping losses, mixture control, catalyst temperature, and emissions limits.

Modern gasoline engines often use direct injection, turbocharging, variable valve timing, exhaust-gas recirculation, and close-coupled catalysts to manage those constraints. Each technology shifts the engineering problem rather than removing it.

## Common Misconceptions

Gasoline engine does not mean carbureted engine. Carburetors were historically important, but electronic fuel injection and engine controls became central to modern gasoline operation. Gasoline engine also does not mean the same thing as four-stroke engine; many gasoline road engines use the four-stroke cycle, but the cycle and the fuel/ignition method are separate concepts.
