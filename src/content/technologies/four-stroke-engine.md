## Definition

A four-stroke engine is a reciprocating piston engine that completes intake, compression, power, and exhaust strokes across two crankshaft revolutions. In spark-ignition automotive use, an air-fuel mixture is compressed and ignited by a spark plug.

The four strokes are a sequence, not four separate machines. The piston, cylinder, valves, crankshaft, camshaft, fuel system, ignition system, cooling system, lubrication system, and exhaust all have to support the same repeating cycle.

## How It Works

The piston moves inside a cylinder and turns a crankshaft through a connecting rod. A simplified power relationship is:

$$
P = T \omega
$$

where $P$ is power, $T$ is torque, and $\omega$ is angular speed.

The cycle can be described as:

1. **Intake:** The piston moves down while the intake valve is open, drawing in air or an air-fuel mixture depending on the fuel system.
2. **Compression:** The piston moves up with valves closed, raising pressure and temperature.
3. **Power:** Combustion expands gases and pushes the piston down, turning the crankshaft.
4. **Exhaust:** The piston moves up again while the exhaust valve is open, pushing burned gases out.

This repeating cycle explains why engine performance is not only about displacement. Valve timing, compression ratio, air flow, fuel metering, ignition timing, friction, cooling, and exhaust backpressure all affect the useful torque delivered to the drivetrain.

## Components and Supporting Systems

A gasoline four-stroke engine needs several supporting systems:

- fuel storage and metering, through carburetion, port injection, or direct injection
- ignition hardware and control
- air intake and filtration
- cooling and lubrication
- exhaust routing and aftertreatment
- mechanical connection to a clutch, torque converter, transmission, or hybrid system

DOE AFDC gasoline-vehicle material is useful because it places the engine inside a complete vehicle component chain. The engine is the prime mover, but it is not the whole powertrain.

## Operating Limits

Only one of the four strokes produces direct expansion work, while the other strokes prepare or clear the cylinder. That makes pumping loss, friction, heat transfer, and incomplete combustion central to real efficiency. The cooling system must reject heat that cannot become useful shaft work, and the lubrication system must reduce friction without contaminating the intake or exhaust.

Engine speed also creates constraints. Higher speed can increase power if torque remains available, but it gives valves, flame travel, bearings, and piston rings less time to do their jobs. Lower speed can improve some losses but may create vibration, knock limits, or poor airflow. The cycle is simple to name and difficult to optimize across a vehicle's full operating range.

## Historical Role

The four-stroke gasoline engine became central to early automobiles because it could be packaged in a road vehicle and supplied by liquid fuel. The Benz Patent-Motorwagen is one early case study in vehicle-level integration, while later mass-production examples show how engines became standardized products.

## Evidence Boundary

The cited sources support the gasoline-vehicle system overview and an early vehicle example. They do not establish one ideal compression ratio, valve layout, or fuel system. AutoEvolve uses the four-stroke cycle as a technical foundation, then leaves detailed engine-family comparisons to more specific pages.

Its later development involved better fuel metering, ignition, cooling, lubrication, emissions control, and electronic management. Modern four-stroke engines therefore should not be imagined as purely mechanical descendants of early engines. They are controlled systems connected to sensors, catalysts, diagnostics, transmissions, and sometimes electric machines.

## Neutral Reading

This page does not frame the four-stroke engine as obsolete, inevitable, or universally preferable. It became dominant for many historical reasons: liquid-fuel energy density, manufacturability, repair networks, road infrastructure, and incremental engineering improvement. Its disadvantages, including exhaust emissions, heat loss, noise, vibration, and fuel dependence, are part of the same technical history.
