## Definition

Vertical integration in EV manufacturing is the strategy of bringing more of
the vehicle system under one organization: cells, packs, motors, power
electronics, software, body structure, final assembly, charging services, or
materials supply. It is not an automatic virtue. It is a tradeoff between
control, capital intensity, flexibility, supplier specialization, and execution
risk.

## Why EVs Changed The Question

Battery electric vehicles concentrate cost and technical risk in batteries,
power electronics, motors, thermal systems, software, and charging. That makes
some manufacturers more interested in controlling the pack architecture,
platform, cell supply, and software lifecycle.

The strategy can be summarized as:

$$
\text{Integration value} = \text{coordination benefit} - \text{execution burden}
$$

The burden matters. Owning more steps means more factories, more process
knowledge, more supply exposure, and more responsibility when something fails.

## Example Boundary

BYD is cited here because its e-Platform 3.0 source explicitly discusses
platform integration and vertical integration. That makes it a useful example
for how battery, platform, and vehicle design can be discussed together. The
page does not treat BYD as a ranking benchmark or endorsement.

## Supply-Chain Context

IEA EV outlook sources frame battery production and supply-chain concentration.
DOE sources frame manufacturing capacity and material supply chains as policy
and industrial resilience questions. Together they show why integration is not
only a factory layout issue; it reaches upstream into cells, materials, and
recycling.

## Evidence Boundary

The cited sources support platform, supply-chain, and battery-production
context. They do not prove that vertical integration is preferable to a
specialist supplier network in every region or vehicle class.
