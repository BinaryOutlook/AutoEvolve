## Summary

Dieselgate refers to regulatory findings and legal proceedings involving diesel vehicles that used software behavior regulators identified as affecting emissions-control operation. The case is important because it connects combustion chemistry, aftertreatment hardware, software calibration, laboratory testing, and real-world emissions.

## Technical Background

Diesel engines often operate lean, with excess air relative to the fuel. Lean combustion can support efficiency, but high combustion temperature and oxygen availability can increase NOx formation. NOx control may involve exhaust gas recirculation, diesel particulate filters, lean NOx traps, and selective catalytic reduction.

## Software and Testing

Modern diesel emissions control is not only a hardware problem. Engine control software decides when and how systems operate. Regulatory test cycles define standardized conditions, but real-world driving varies in temperature, speed, load, altitude, and driver behavior.

## Regulatory Context

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency documented Volkswagen diesel violations and later described civil settlement terms. The case made defeat-device rules and real-world emissions testing more visible to the public.

## Historical Significance

Dieselgate belongs in automotive technology history because it showed that software had become a powertrain component with regulatory consequences. It also affected public trust in passenger diesel technology and strengthened attention on test-cycle design and in-use emissions.
