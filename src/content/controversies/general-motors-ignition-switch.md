## Summary

General Motors' 2014 ignition switch crisis concerned a small mechanical part with system-wide safety consequences. The switch in certain vehicles could move out of the run position with low torque. When that happened, the engine could stall, electrical power could be reduced, and airbag deployment logic could be affected because the vehicle was no longer in the expected operating state.

NHTSA's 2014 consent order found that GM failed to notify the agency in a timely way after learning of a safety-related defect. The Department of Justice later alleged that GM concealed the defect from NHTSA and the public, and GM entered a deferred-prosecution agreement. GM also commissioned the Valukas report, an internal investigation that described organizational fragmentation, committee handoffs, and failure to connect field incidents with an urgent defect decision.

## Historical Context

By the 2000s, even inexpensive ignition switches had become gateways into more complex vehicle systems. A driver's key position no longer controlled only a simple starter circuit. It affected power availability, engine operation, diagnostic state, and restraint-system readiness.

This case emerged during a period when automakers were using increasingly networked electrical architectures while still relying on many legacy mechanical interfaces. The switch was a mechanical component, but the consequence of switch movement reached electronic modules and crash-protection logic.

## What Technically or Procedurally Failed

The technical issue was low detent torque. In ordinary language, the key could be easier to rotate out of the run position than intended. Added weight on a key ring, knee contact, or road vibration could contribute to movement in some circumstances.

The procedural failure was the more historically significant layer. Field reports, engineering discussions, service experience, and legal information did not produce a timely recall decision. The Valukas report described a pattern in which information was distributed across groups but not escalated with sufficient ownership.

A simplified systems view is:

$$
\text{Safety Outcome} = \text{Component Robustness} \times \text{Signal Detection} \times \text{Recall Governance}
$$

The switch mattered, but the governance system determined how long the hazard remained in use.

## Findings and Responses

NHTSA's consent order required GM to pay a civil penalty and make changes to safety-decision processes. The order also required additional reporting, training, and access for NHTSA's monitor-like oversight functions. NHTSA attributed the enforcement action to delayed defect reporting.

The DOJ alleged wire-fraud and concealment theories, then resolved the matter through a deferred-prosecution agreement. GM agreed to forfeit funds, accept compliance obligations, and cooperate with an independent monitor. The DOJ resolution did not replace civil claims or NHTSA's safety role; it addressed alleged criminal conduct in communications and disclosure.

GM's commissioned Valukas report identified multiple missed opportunities. It found that employees often treated the matter as a customer-satisfaction or engineering issue rather than a safety defect with airbag implications. GM stated that it accepted the report's findings and announced personnel and process changes.

## Consequences

The crisis had direct safety consequences for crashes in which power loss and airbag nondeployment were alleged factors. It also reshaped defect-reporting expectations. NHTSA's enforcement response emphasized that a manufacturer must act when it has enough information to identify a safety defect; perfect certainty is not the threshold for notification.

For public trust, the case demonstrated how a low-cost component can become a major safety crisis when organizational signals are weak. It also influenced how manufacturers structure safety offices, escalation procedures, and documentation around field data.

## What This Case Teaches

The ignition switch case teaches that modern safety depends on both engineering validation and institutional memory. A component with inadequate operating margin may be discovered by testing, warranty data, litigation, complaints, or crash investigations. If those signals are separated, the vehicle system remains exposed.

The case also illustrates why recall governance is a technical topic. A defect decision is not only a legal filing; it is the mechanism that turns distributed evidence into a remedy for vehicles already on the road.
